# African CITES Wood

Overview of CITES-protected wood from the African region, from the [[CITES Timber ID]] repository (list as in force after CoP20; amendments in force 5 March 2026). Ask Jarvis things like "which African woods are CITES listed", "how do I tell bubinga from rosewood", "what annotation does Khaya have" or "what papers does African padauk need". Species details: [[African CITES Wood Species]].

**African taxa on the CITES lists (all Appendix II)**
- **Dalbergia** (rosewoods, hongmu, African blackwood or mpingo, Madagascar rosewood): annotation #15. The listing covers the whole genus worldwide; Dalbergia nigra from Brazil is Appendix I.
- **Guibourtia demeusei, G. pellegriniana, G. tessmannii** (bubinga, kevazingo, African rosewood): annotation #15.
- **Pterocarpus**: the African populations of the genus (African padauk, muninga, mukwa, kiaat) are listed with annotation #17 since 2023. Pterocarpus erinaceus (kosso, West African rosewood or hongmu) has no annotation. Pterocarpus tinctorius (mukula) has #6, and for African populations also #17.
- **Khaya** (African mahogany), African populations: #17.
- **Afzelia** (doussié, pod mahogany, chanfuta), African populations: #17.
- **Pericopsis elata** (afrormosia, assamela): #17.
- **Diospyros** (ebony), Madagascar populations only: #5.
- **Osyris lanceolata** (East African sandalwood), listed populations of Burundi, Ethiopia, Kenya, Rwanda, Uganda and Tanzania: #2.
- **Widdringtonia whytei** (Mulanje cedar, Malawi): no annotation.
- **Prunus africana** (African cherry, pygeum): #4. Mainly traded as medicinal bark, not timber.
- Not wood but listed: Adansonia grandidieri (baobab seeds and oil) and Commiphora wightii (guggul). Boswellia and other resin trees are not CITES-listed.
- CoP20 (2025) rejected proposals to remove Afzelia bipindensis and Pterocarpus soyauxii from the lists, so they stay listed.

**Annotations explained (what products need a CITES document)**
- No annotation: all parts and products, including finished goods.
- #2: all parts and derivatives except seeds and pollen, and finished products packaged for retail.
- #4: all parts and derivatives except seeds, pollen and a few plant-specific items; for timber, logs, sawn wood, veneer, plywood and finished products are covered.
- #5: logs, sawn wood and veneer sheets only.
- #6: logs, sawn wood, veneer sheets and plywood.
- #15: all parts and derivatives except leaves, flowers, pollen, fruits and seeds, finished products up to 10 kg of wood per shipment, and finished musical instruments.
- #17: logs, sawn wood, veneer sheets, plywood and transformed wood (HS 44.09, such as decking and flooring strips).

**Documents (Appendix II)**
- An export permit from the exporting country, or a re-export certificate. CITES itself does not require an import permit, but many countries do, for example the EU.
- The permit is valid six months, shows a unique number, stamp and signature, and covers one consignment. Species, quantity and origin must match the shipment.
- Population limits matter: Khaya, Afzelia and Pterocarpus count only from Africa, and Diospyros only from Madagascar.
- Source code W means taken from the wild. A means artificially propagated, so verify a plantation claim. O means pre-Convention, which needs a certificate.

**Red flags in trade**
- Round logs or squared beams of red rosewood out of West Africa: usually Pterocarpus erinaceus, the main species in the illegal West African hongmu trade.
- Red logs leaving southern or eastern Africa for Asia: treat as suspect mukula (Pterocarpus tinctorius) or red sanders. Mukula is often traded as "red sandalwood".
- Bubinga slabs and kevazingo veneer from Cameroon, Gabon and Equatorial Guinea.
- Madagascar rosewood and ebony: Madagascar has an export ban and CITES zero-quota guidance, so any shipment is a serious red flag.
- Small fragrant yellowish logs or roots from East Africa: Osyris sandalwood, smuggled for oil distillation.
- Wrong declaration: African timber declared as a similar unlisted species, for example unlisted sapele instead of Khaya, or unlisted ovangkol instead of bubinga.

**Telling woods apart with a 10x hand lens**
- Ripple marks (storied structure) on the side face: present in Dalbergia, Pterocarpus and Pericopsis. Absent in Guibourtia (bubinga) and Khaya.
- Yellow or whitish deposits in large pores point to Afzelia, but unlisted Asian merbau and African iroko look similar.
- Mahogany look with no ripple marks and interlocked grain: Khaya. Unlisted sapele, sipo and kosipo (Entandrophragma) are the look-alikes.
- Teak-coloured but diffuse-porous with ripple marks: afrormosia, not teak.
- Jet black, very heavy wood: Madagascar ebony or African blackwood. Origin decides.
- Quick checks: a water test on shavings gives a red or orange colour for red Pterocarpus such as P. tinctorius; a heartwood chip that sinks suggests very dense wood such as African blackwood.
- Hand-lens identification reaches genus or species group only. Take samples for a laboratory (wood anatomy, DNA, DART-TOF mass spectrometry, NIR).

Always check the live listing and annotation on Species+ and with the national CITES Management Authority. This is a screening aid, not legal advice or a final identification.
